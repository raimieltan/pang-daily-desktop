# **Prologue**

## ***The Missing Seconds***

The first thing Pang remembered was the sound of rain striking the windshield as if someone were throwing handfuls of gravel at it.

The wipers dragged water from side to side. Their rubber edges had worn unevenly, leaving a pale blur across the glass that returned every second, right in the middle of his sightline. Beyond it, the mountain road shone black beneath the headlights. The yellow center markings came and went under the hood. The engine was loud enough to flatten every other sound in the world.

Jolo had his phone raised near the passenger window. He was still recording. He recorded everything: the good runs, the bad ones, the small arguments in the garage, the moments when Pang insisted a noise was nothing and Ruel insisted that noises did not become harmless just because a man ignored them.

“Feels different here,” Jolo said, thin through the engine note. Then: “It's wet past the sign.”

The corner appeared. Pang knew it by the shape of the trees and the pale concrete post on the outside. He knew the sequence before it arrived because he had watched it so many times: the entry, the shallow turn, the late sweep toward the exit. Ghost's line. The perfect line.

A brightness moved between the trees.

For an instant, Pang thought another car was beside them. The light slid across the wet glass at an impossible angle, close enough to make the road feel narrower than it was. He looked toward it and back. The headlights waited in the edge of his vision like eyes that had found him.

“Pang.” Jolo's voice. “Pang, it's wet. It's wet.”

Pang was already committed. He braked. The car's nose dipped. The rear moved in a way he had felt before, on other corners, on other nights, and his hands answered before the rest of him could think. A sharp correction. The steering wheel pushed back. The tires lost the surface that had been holding them a second earlier.

There was no clean instant when the car stopped obeying him. It happened in increments: the wheel growing light, the painted line moving sideways, the road no longer arriving from the direction he expected. Jolo's phone flashed white as it caught the headlights. The world turned toward the trees.

Pang heard Jolo say, very quietly, “Okay. Okay.”

Then the windshield filled with light.

Afterward, he would remember the impact as silence. Not the sound of metal folding or glass breaking, but an abrupt absence where the world had been. A silence so complete it seemed to have happened before the crash, as if the road had already taken everything away and the car was only catching up.

For months, he would try to recover the seconds that came before it. He would remember the headlights, the white smear of rain, and Jolo saying something like *drive the road*. He would remember that he had reacted to something beside him. He would not remember exactly what he saw, where the other car was, or whether there had even been a car on the same road.

In his mind, the headlights would always arrive at the corner.

The rest would be missing.

# **Chapter One**

## ***The Sound of a Wrench***

The wrench hit the concrete with a flat, bright crack.

Pang woke from a sleep he had not realized he was in. For a moment he was back on the mountain, the headlights coming between the trees. Then the smell of oil and hot dust settled over him, and Ruel bent to pick up the wrench as if nothing important had happened.

“You're staring at it again,” Jolo said.

He was sitting on an upside-down plastic pail beside the car, eating a pan de sal from a paper bag and pointing the bread at Pang like a teacher's ruler. His phone was propped against a jar of bolts, its camera trained on the open hood. The garage was barely wide enough to fit the car and three people without someone turning sideways. Tools hung from a board above the workbench. A faded calendar curled at the corners. Beyond the open roller door, the city had begun its ordinary afternoon argument: motorcycles, tricycles, horns, a vendor calling out the day's fruit.

“Thinking,” Pang said.

“Twenty minutes, boss. Hood's still up.”

Ruel leaned over the engine bay. He did not smile, but the corner of his mouth moved. “When the philosophers are done. Ten-millimeter.”

Pang passed it over. The car was old enough that every repair seemed to uncover another repair. Its paint had faded into a color that could not decide whether it was blue or gray. The driver's seat had a split seam. A strip of black tape held one side of the console together. The engine, which Pang knew by sound better than he knew most people's voices, had been rebuilt in pieces over years of overtime and favors.

Ruel had kept it alive through a combination of thrift, skill, and refusal to be impressed. He could tell Pang the state of a part without looking at it. He could also tell when Pang was trying to turn a mechanical problem into a question of pride.

“Fronts are near the end,” Ruel said. “And I don't like the pedal.”

“They're not done.”

“They are not finished becoming dangerous. Different thing.”

Pang looked toward Jolo, who had suddenly become fascinated by the bread in his hand.

“Tires next week,” Jolo said. “Sooner if tonight goes good.”

“What's tonight,” Ruel asked.

“Small meet. Private. Prize covers the parts.”

“Some of the parts,” Ruel corrected.

Jolo spread his hands. “ ‘Some’ is how ‘more’ begins.”

Ruel set the wrench down carefully this time. “That is also how debt begins.”

Jolo's smile returned, but it was smaller. He had a way of hearing a warning and immediately searching it for an opening. He knew which shop owner would let them pay later, which racer had a spare wheel, who had a cousin in a parts warehouse, and which promises could be stretched across two paydays. It was how he made life move when money refused to.

Pang knew the risks. He also knew what the car felt like when everything came together. The engine pulled cleanly; the front tires spoke through the steering wheel; the body settled just enough through a corner to tell him where its weight had gone. Sometimes, for three or four seconds, he felt less like a man making choices and more like a person remembering something his body had always known.

That feeling was the reason he kept coming back.

Jolo had plans beyond the next race. He wanted a garage with their name on it, a small shop that could also shoot and edit videos for other drivers. He wanted to turn knowing everybody into something that paid. He talked about a future as if it were a place they could reach if they found the right road.

Pang wanted the same things, though he said them less often. What he wanted most was to stop having every ambition measured against the cost of a part.

Ruel looked at them both, then at the tires. “Go,” he said. “Me not stopping you is not me saying yes.”

Jolo stood and clapped once. “That's almost a blessing.”

“It's an invoice.”

Pang lowered the hood. The latch caught on the first try. It was a small victory, and he took it as one.

# **Chapter Two**

## ***Nothing to Lose***

The race began behind a row of warehouses where the pavement changed from clean concrete to patched asphalt. No one called it a street race. They called it a meet, a run, a private event, as though a different word could make the risks behave differently.

Cars gathered under white work lights. Their owners stood in loose groups with paper cups of coffee, comparing wheels and listening to engines idle. Some builds were immaculate. Others looked like Pang's: assembled by someone who understood exactly which parts could be postponed and which parts would eventually refuse to wait.

Jolo moved through the crowd like he had been invited by every person there. He greeted a man who sold used wheels, teased a driver whose car had been in the shop for a month, and found the organizer to confirm that Pang's entry fee had been accepted. By the time Pang rolled to the start, Jolo had collected three new contacts and a promise of a discounted alignment.

“Bring it home in one piece,” Ruel had texted before they left.

Jolo had answered, “Define home.” Ruel had not laughed.

The other cars sounded expensive. Their engines rose in clean, confident bursts, and their drivers wore the composed expressions of people who had spent money preparing for the possibility of failure. Pang's car rattled at idle. A loose bit of trim tapped against the dash. He could feel the vibration through the seat and knew which note belonged to the exhaust hanger and which belonged to a heat shield that had never fit correctly.

The *starter* raised one hand.

For a moment the world reduced itself to the road in front of him. The crowd became a blur at the edge of the windshield. The car beside him launched cleanly, its tires catching with a chirp. Pang followed a fraction later, not because he hesitated but because he had learned the car's throttle well enough to know what the first instant of movement would cost.

He lost ground on the straight. The better-powered cars walked away from him whenever the road opened. But the route narrowed into a sequence of bends and short transitions, and there the contest changed. He felt the weight shift before he could have explained it. He lifted. The front end settled. He turned once, then eased the wheel back as the car carried itself toward the exit.

A driver ahead braked early and left space. Pang found a way through—not a miracle, just a small piece of road used well. He gained a length, then another. His heart beat hard enough to make each breath feel separate.

At the final bend, the rear moved outward. His hands made the familiar quick adjustment, a short correction that brought the car back without asking it to do more than it could. He crossed the finish a little ahead of the driver everyone had expected to win.

The crowd's surprise reached him before the result did. Someone slapped the roof as he slowed. A few people laughed in disbelief. Jolo was yelling before the car had fully stopped, waving both arms, the camera still recording.

Pang climbed out and tried not to grin too much. The prize was not life-changing money. It was enough to matter: enough for the tire bill to look possible, enough for the next week to feel less like a problem already arriving.

“You saw that?” Jolo said, as if he had not filmed all of it. “You see what you did?”

“I drove.”

“You made that car look like it owes you money.”

Pang looked back at the finish. He could still feel the last correction in his wrists. It had worked. On that road, with those tires, under those conditions, it had worked.

A man in a dark overshirt approached with the relaxed pace of someone who knew he would be given room. Dante was not the fastest driver there, but he knew every driver who mattered and several who only thought they did. He congratulated Pang, asked the right questions about the car, and listened as though the answers could become useful.

“You found time where there wasn't supposed to be any,” Dante said.

Pang glanced at Jolo. His friend was smiling like the future had just opened a door.

Dante looked at the ordinary car, the cheap tires, and the people still talking about the finish. “Come by Kyo, my friend,” he said. “Meet some people. Coffee's on me.”

# **Chapter Three**

## ***A Man Who Arranged Things***

Dante's gift was not speed. It was making people feel that the next room had been waiting for them.

He took Pang and Jolo to a coffee shop that stayed open past midnight, where modified cars filled the parking spaces and the windows reflected headlamps in long white bands. Some drivers came to talk about builds. Others came to be seen talking about builds. Dante knew which was which. He moved between tables, touching shoulders, remembering old arguments, asking after a person's mother or a broken gearbox. When he introduced Pang, he did not call him a charity case or a discovery. He called him the driver who had surprised everyone at the warehouse run.

The difference mattered. Pang felt it land.

Jolo handled the practical side. He asked about entry fees, the rules at the next event, and whether there would be a media pass for the runs. He turned every introduction into a possible future. Dante never promised much outright. He offered access, which was more powerful because it looked like a gift and behaved like a loan.

“Look— people want to watch you,” Dante told Pang. “Small car. No budget. You.”

“I just drive.”

Pang could hear the salesmanship in it, but he also understood the appeal. For years, money had been the wall between what he knew how to do and what he was allowed to attempt. Dante seemed to know where the wall had a door.

They met Nico that evening. His car was clean in a way Pang's never could be: paint deep enough to hold the parking lot lights, wheels without a nick, an engine bay arranged as if every wire had been asked to justify itself. Nico listened while someone retold Pang's win, then looked at the car and asked what tires he was running.

“Three,” Pang said.

Nico nodded, as though that confirmed something. “You drove well.”

The compliment sounded more insulting than an insult would have. Pang asked whether Nico wanted to say something else.

“But you found time you won't find twice. Not on those.” Nico's tone remained even.

Jolo made a small sound that was supposed to be a cough.

Nico did not sneer. That made it worse. He spoke as if the distinction were simple: preparation, repeatability, the ability to do a thing again after the first lucky attempt had worn off. Pang hated him immediately for being right in a way that sounded like privilege.

Dante stepped between them with a joke and turned the conversation elsewhere. It was effortless. One moment Pang was ready to start an argument; the next, three people were discussing a delayed shipment of parts.

Later, on the drive home, Jolo kept talking about the people they'd met. “Do you realize who Dante introduced us to? That shop in Mandurriao could get us a used set of wheels. And the guy who does wraps knows somebody looking for video work. We could actually start building something.”

Pang watched the road unspool beneath the headlights. “Nico thinks we got lucky.”

“Nico thinks everyone is a budget line item.”

“He wasn't wrong about the car.”

Jolo went quiet for a moment. Then he reached over and tapped the dash. “Then we'll make a car he can't dismiss.”

It sounded possible because Jolo said it as if possibility were a thing he could negotiate with.

In the days that followed, Dante's invitations became more frequent. The events were better organized, the drivers faster, the stakes larger. They made some money and spent almost all of it on the car. Pang learned that a better room rarely made a person less broke. It simply gave him more expensive ways to discover it.

# **Chapter Four**

## ***Nico’s Lesson***

Nico beat him by less than a second.

That was how Pang remembered it afterward: not the start, not the crowd, not the clean finish, only the number on the screen and Nico standing beside his car with his helmet under one arm. Less than a second was too small a gap to feel like defeat and too large a gap to dismiss as bad luck.

For the first part of the run, Pang had driven beautifully. The car felt alive, and the corners came in a rhythm he could almost hear before they arrived. He took time out of Nico on the narrow section, where the other man's larger advantage could not fully express itself. For a few moments, Pang saw the race from ahead.

Then the route opened. Nico's car put its power down cleanly. He did not rush the entry to reclaim what he had lost. He kept the car balanced, made no dramatic corrections, and carried the same pace through the next sequence. Pang tried to answer with instinct. His line tightened. He asked the front tires for a little more than he had asked them a moment before, and the car responded, but not for free.

By the finish, Nico was ahead.

“You're quick,” Nico said when Pang found him in the paddock area. “You take every corner like it's personal.”

Pang pulled off his gloves. “You drive like there's a spreadsheet open in your head.”

“There is.” Nico glanced at his phone. “First pass, second pass, tire temperature, entry speed. Yours has one row.”

“Must be nice. Ten sets of tires.”

Nico accepted the hit. “It is.” He paused. “Tires aren't why you overcorrected at the last bend. You did it because the car moved and you wanted to fix it before it finished moving.”

Pang had no answer that would not sound like a denial.

Jolo joined them with two bottles of water and a grin that was trying hard to bring the old mood back. “Good race. Really good race. Footage, contacts, all four tires.” Then, lower: “Don't tell Ruel about the tires.”

The joke died in the air before it reached the three of them. Jolo looked down at the bottles as if they had betrayed him.

Later, he edited the race at a table in the garage. He cut out the parts where Pang lost time and kept the moments where the car looked almost impossibly nimble. He was good at this: finding the clean image inside a messy event. He understood that a video could make five seconds feel like a life turning point. He knew which angle made a modest car look fierce and which engine note would make a viewer lean closer.

“I'll make a clip that shows what you can do,” he said.

Pang stood behind him, arms crossed. “Just don't make me look like I got beaten.”

“You got beaten.”

“Then don't make it obvious.”

Jolo laughed and nudged him with an elbow. The sound filled the garage, and for a while the loss became another story they could tell without flinching.

Over the next several weeks, Pang drove the route in practice sessions, learning where the car was honest and where his own confidence made him careless. Jolo recorded from the passenger seat, from the roadside, from anywhere he could safely stand. Ruel insisted that they log changes to the car. Nico returned to repeat his runs, sometimes with a friend, sometimes alone. He treated every pass as a question that deserved more than one answer.

Pang still disliked him. But he began to watch how Nico drove.

The lesson was not that talent did not matter. It was that talent could be spent badly, and Pang had a habit of spending it all at once.

# **Chapter Five**

## ***The Line Study***

The Ghost Line began with a joke.

They were on the mountain road in the late afternoon, when the light sat low between the trees and the asphalt changed color every time a cloud passed over the sun. They had made three passes already. On the first, halfway down, Jolo had said, “You're doing the thing. Your mouth goes small. Drive the road, boss. It's right there.” Pang had not answered. On the second, the inside of the bend was still damp from a shower, and Jolo had asked if he felt it. Pang's best entry came from the first run. The middle section was cleaner on the second. On the third, the exit was fast enough that Jolo leaned back in his seat and said, “There. That's the one.”

“Which one?” Pang asked.

“The one we'll pretend you meant to do.”

“My mouth doesn't go small.”

“I have eleven hours of your mouth going small.”

“One more time,” Pang said.

“You said that three times ago.” Jolo pointed at the trees. “Light's gone, boss.”

Pang laughed. They turned around and went back to the start.

The footage was ordinary in the way good footage often was before someone decided it mattered. A tire brushed over a seam in the road. Pang lifted at a corner, a little too early. He corrected the car at the bend near the broken sign. On another pass, the road marker stood intact. On a later one, a delivery truck had left a dark wet streak across the asphalt. The engine note rose and fell with each attempt, and between the best moments there were pauses, resets, and small failures.

At the garage that night, Jolo lined the clips up on his laptop. Pang watched from the other side of the workbench while Ruel pretended not to listen.

“What if you took the entry from this one,” Jolo said, scrubbing backward, “the middle from the second, and the exit from today? It'd show the line you keep reaching for.”

“Would it be cheating?”

“It's not a record. It's a reference.” Jolo glanced up. “A study. Like putting your best pieces together to see what you're aiming at.”

He made the cuts. He matched the engine sound across the transitions, smoothing a brief dip until the car seemed to move through the road without hesitation. When he was done, the route flowed in one unbroken gesture. Pang watched the car move through a corner he remembered correcting badly, then through another where the front tires had finally found the right balance, then out of a section that had taken them four attempts to get clean.

The edit looked like a promise.

Jolo named the file GHOST\_LINE\_COMPOSITE. Beneath it, in his notes, he typed: \*\*COMPOSITE LINE STUDY — BEST SECTORS / NOT ONE RUN.\*\* He added the sector times from the strongest passes, then combined them into a total target to compare against future attempts. The combined time was not a lap record. It was a measurement of the best work they'd done in separate pieces.

Pang leaned toward the screen. “That looks like a driver who knows exactly what he's doing.”

“You do know what you're doing.”

“Not like that.”

Jolo's expression softened. “Not yet.”

He exported the clip without the notes, intending to send it to a small group of drivers who might appreciate it. The edit was meant to be a tool, not a monument. It would let Pang study his own best moments without needing to drive another five times every time he wanted to remember them.

Dante messaged before midnight.

\*\*Who made this?\*\*

Jolo stared at the question. Pang, beside him, was already replaying the clip.

“Tell him it's ours,” Pang said.

Jolo looked at the smooth cut from the entry to the middle. On the screen, there was no pause, no wrong line, no reset. It was only their car moving through the road with uncanny confidence.

“It's a line study,” he said.

“I know.” Pang's eyes stayed on the video. “But it's good.”

Jolo sent Dante the file. The note went with it, at least at first. For a few minutes, the project remained what it had been: an honest study of a driver learning what his car could do.

# **Chapter Six**

## ***Ghost Goes Public***

By the next afternoon, the clip had a different name.

Dante had posted it to a private group, then to another group, then to the account used to announce his events. The notes had vanished. The title card had been removed. The caption called it a reference pace set by an anonymous driver, a line so clean that nobody in the local scene had been able to match it. Someone in the comments called the unseen driver Ghost. The name stuck because it was short, mysterious, and easy to put on a poster.

Jolo found out at a coffee shop while he was checking messages for a cheap set of wheels. A video had been reposted with a new overlay: a total time, a sequence of sector targets, and the words \*\*GHOST TIME\*\*. The figure was almost the combined best-sector time from their separate practice runs. Almost: it sat a touch under the sum in his own notes, as if someone had rounded it toward the impossible before putting it up. It looked precise enough to be official.

He watched it twice. On the second pass, he noticed the missing title card.

“Dante took something off it,” he said.

Pang leaned across the table. The clip played between them. “Off what? It looks good.”

“Yeah. It looks really good.”

Jolo opened the original project folder, then closed it again. He could message Dante. He could post the source file and explain the edit. He could tell the group that Ghost was not a driver and the total was not a completed performance. The truth was simple. It would take less than a minute to type.

But the group was already talking about them. People wanted to know what car Ghost drove. One person claimed the line belonged to a former racer who had moved abroad. Another said the driver had disappeared after a crash. Someone asked whether the next event would feature an attempt to beat the time.

“People are into it. Somebody out there can drive,” Pang said quietly. He didn't sound greedy. He sounded surprised, as if the scene had finally seen something he had been trying to show it for years.

Jolo thought of the unpaid shop bill and the tires. He thought of the garage they kept talking about, the one with their name above the door. He told himself that Dante's exaggeration would burn out. Anyone who understood driving would see the difference between a reference study and a record. If the attention got them into better events, they could correct the story later, once the opportunity had done its work.

He did not correct it.

Dante called that evening. “The scene needs a benchmark,” he said. “Something people can chase. Nobody cares about a clip labeled 'study.' They care about a driver nobody can catch.”

“It's not one run.”

“I know what it is.” Dante's voice held no shame, only impatience with a distinction he considered unhelpful. “I haven't called it an official record. It's an unofficial pace. Everyone understands what a legend is.”

“They're putting a time on it.”

“That's what makes it interesting.”

Dante explained that the combined sectors gave the event something measurable. Drivers could attempt the first section, then the next, then the last. The leaderboard could show individual bests and the combined target. If someone matched one part, the rest would remain. If nobody matched the whole thing, the myth would grow. He spoke as if he had found a clever format, not a way to make a false premise profitable.

Jolo looked at Pang, who was watching the clip on his phone again.

“People are going to try it,” Jolo said.

“Of course. That's the point.”

When the call ended, the Ghost Line already belonged to people who had never seen Jolo's working notes. The first public challenge was announced before the week was out. The legend had acquired a time, a name, and a reason to survive.

# **Chapter Seven**

## ***The Time That Would Not Fit***

The first time Pang tried to beat Ghost, he got one sector right and lost the rest of the run.

He had watched the clip so often that the line seemed to occupy the road before the road itself appeared. He knew where the ghost car would turn, how long it would hold its angle, where its speed would carry it toward the exit. The overlay was clean and pale, a second vehicle made from light. It left no tire noise and asked for no correction.

Pang's real car did not feel like that. It loaded its front tires, changed balance as the road rose and fell, and reminded him that every turn had a cost. He matched the Ghost Line through the first sector. At the transition, he was close enough to imagine the target shrinking. Then he entered the middle section with the rhythm he'd just used, and the car asked for a different approach. By the time he reached the exit, the composite pace had pulled away.

He sat in the car after the run, hands still on the wheel.

“It's a clip, boss,” Jolo said from the passenger seat. “You don't have to make it nervous.”

Pang gave him a look. “It has a time.”

“It's a time built out of the best parts of different runs.” The words came out too quickly. Jolo tried to make them sound casual. “That's what a reference is.”

Pang turned toward him. “You think I don't know that? I'm trying to get close.”

Jolo looked away. “I know.”

At the next event, Dante displayed the Ghost target across a tall screen beside the staging area. Drivers queued to attempt individual sectors. They could win one and still fail the total, because the total assumed that the best entry, middle, and exit could all be combined in a single pass. Nico ran two controlled attempts and matched one of the sector times, then stopped to review his notes.

“Sector one wants one entry,” he said when Pang reached him. “The middle was recorded on a different surface. The exit's from a pass with a different rhythm. I can drive every piece. They don't add up.”

Pang looked at the leaderboard. A small line of text beneath the official overlay still read \*\*COMBINED BEST SECTORS\*\*. It disappeared when the next promotional slide came up.

“Then make them add up,” Pang said.

Nico studied him for a moment. “What do you think I've been doing.”

Dante turned every failed attempt into an advertisement for Ghost. If a driver came close, he called it progress. If a driver missed the target, he called it proof of the benchmark's difficulty. The more the totals failed to match, the more valuable the legend became.

After midnight, Pang found Jolo sitting in the car with his phone open. The project notes were on the screen. Jolo had typed a message to Pang and not sent it. Beneath it was the original label: \*\*NOT ONE RUN.\*\*

“There is something about the clip I should've—” Jolo began.

Pang turned from the windshield, excited and exhausted. “I know. I know it's hard. If I get the middle clean, the rest follow.”

Jolo looked at his face. He saw not arrogance but hope, the same hope he had spent months trying to feed. He could still tell him. He could say that the clip was a construction and that there was no complete performance waiting at the end of all this effort.

Instead, he locked his phone.

“You remember the batchoy place in La Paz?” he said after a while. “By the school?”

“It closed.”

“It didn't close. I just never went back. I knew it wouldn't taste like that again, so I kept it how it was.”

“You're tired,” Pang said.

Jolo looked at something on his phone for a long time. Then he locked it. “Fronts are done, by the way. And it's raining Friday. Ruel's right. I hate when Ruel's right.”

“I almost had the middle tonight.”

“I know.”

Pang heard caution. He did not hear a confession.

# **Chapter Eight**

## ***The Weather Check***

The offer came with a number large enough to make every other number in their lives look small.

Dante wanted a night run on the mountain route. The format would be simple: drivers would attempt to beat the Ghost Line's combined-sector pace, and the best result would take the prize. There would be a larger crowd than usual, private bets, sponsors who wanted their names in the footage, and enough attention to turn the event into the biggest thing Dante had organized that season.

For Pang, the money represented an end to several immediate problems. The car could get proper tires. Ruel could replace the parts he had been telling them not to postpone. They could pay the remaining shop bill, cover what Jolo owed on a camera lens, and still have something left over. It was the sort of chance they had imagined when the car first began winning races.

Ruel looked at the weather report and said no.

“You can say no to Dante,” he told Pang. “You can't say no to standing water.”

The sky over Iloilo had been heavy all afternoon. By dusk, the mountains had disappeared behind a low sheet of rain. Ruel examined the tires, checked the brakes, and explained what he already knew: the car was not prepared for a high-speed run in those conditions. The tires they could afford were acceptable for ordinary driving and dry practice. He would not choose them for a wet mountain route at night.

“We've driven there before,” Pang said.

“Dry. Slower. Different week.”

Jolo said little while they checked the car. When Ruel left to answer a call, Jolo walked with Pang around the front bumper and spoke so quietly that the rain filled the gaps between his words.

“We should pull out.”

Pang stared at him. “You got us in.”

“I got us an invitation. I didn't order the rain.”

“Money changes things.”

“Money doesn't change grip.”

Pang looked at the Ghost Line clip on his phone. The reference car entered the fatal bend with a smooth confidence that seemed almost gentle. It did not show the wet patch near the outside edge. It did not show the shift in the asphalt where the road had been repaired. It did not show how much Pang had needed to correct the car during the real run from which that section had come.

“I'm close,” he said.

Jolo's face changed. He could see that Pang was no longer arguing about the event. He was arguing with the months of effort that had led to it, with every failed attempt, every person who had told him to be patient, every advantage Nico had been able to buy and Pang had not. If he backed out now, the target would remain above him, perfect and untouched.

“Boss,” Jolo said, “the clip isn't—”

His phone buzzed. Dante's name appeared. Jolo stared at it until the vibration stopped.

He had another chance. He did not take it.

Pang interpreted the silence as agreement. “We take the first section easy,” he said. “Feel the road.”

Ruel heard him through the open garage door. “You don't get to feel a wet road after you've committed to the corner.”

Jolo pushed a pan de sal into Pang's hand. “Eat. You brake angry when you're hungry.”

“Bring it home,” Ruel said.

Jolo did not make the joke. “Okay.”

No one answered.

The rain softened, then returned harder. Their deadline did not move. By the time they loaded the car for the mountain, the event was already being announced online as the night someone would finally beat Ghost.

# **Chapter Nine**

## ***The Last Night Run***

The mountain road looked narrower at night.

Headlights cut into mist and found only short pieces of the world: a white line, a guardrail, the shine of wet leaves, the next bend waiting in the dark. Cars were parked at the designated pull-off. People stood under umbrellas and hoods, their faces lit by phones. Dante moved through the gathering, greeting drivers and keeping the event on schedule. He had arranged the night with the care of a man who wanted every angle recorded.

Ruel stood near the car with his arms folded. He did not tell Pang one last time to stay. He had already said everything worth saying, and he understood that another warning would be turned into an obstacle to overcome.

Jolo climbed into the passenger seat with the camera ready. The phone mount was loose, so he wedged it against the window and checked the battery. He looked at Pang, then at the road.

“Do you want to go?” Pang asked.

Jolo could have said no. He could have told Pang everything—the composite, the note, the false time. He could have walked away and let the event happen without them. But his friend was already wearing the expression he had seen before a race: concentrated, hungry, trying to make the future narrow enough to drive through.

“We're here,” Jolo said.

The starter gave them the signal.

For the first section, Pang drove within himself. The engine climbed through the gears. Water hissed under the tires. The road gave him just enough information to stay moving, and he followed it rather than the overlay that lived in his memory. But as the second sector approached, he began to anticipate the Ghost Line. He tried to set the car for the same entry he had watched hundreds of times. The road did not match the recording. The surface was slicker, the light flatter, the corner's edge hidden by rain.

“Feels different here,” Jolo said.

Pang said nothing.

“It's wet past the sign.”

Pang saw the reference line in his head, pale and precise. He was not far from the target. He could feel it in the way the car moved, in the sector time he had calculated before they left, in the thought that this one pass could finally connect all the pieces.

The corner arrived.

He braked later than he had on the dry practice run. The car's weight moved forward, and the tires struggled to hold the line he demanded. The rear began to drift. His hands answered with the familiar correction, a sharp movement that had saved him on other surfaces, at other speeds. Here it unsettled the car further. The tires lost grip entirely.

“Pang.” Jolo's voice, small. “Pang, it's wet. It's wet.” Then, very quietly: “Okay. Okay.”

Headlights slid through the trees to their left. Pang looked, just for an instant, and saw what seemed to be another car coming level with them. The image arrived with the force of a fact. Another driver. Another presence. Someone entering the corner at the exact wrong time.

He corrected again.

The car left the road.

The impact tore the night apart. The camera flew from Jolo's hand. Light flashed through the cabin, then disappeared. Pang heard metal fold around him and something strike his shoulder. After that came a silence too large to understand.

When he opened his eyes, rain was falling through a gap in the broken windshield. The engine was dead. He could not turn his head. He called Jolo's name once, then again, but the passenger seat had become a dark shape tangled with glass and the strap of the camera.

He remembered the headlights in the trees.

He remembered that another car had been there.

The memory would keep those two statements together long after every piece of evidence began to pull them apart.

# **Chapter Ten**

## ***Waking Without a Finish***

Months later, Pang returned to the garage because the hospital had discharged him and the world expected him to continue.

His body had healed in uneven patches. A scar climbed from his collar toward his shoulder. He still woke at sounds that had no place in the room: rain against a window, a wiper dragging over glass, the sudden scrape of a tire losing purchase. His memory of the crash stopped at the headlights. Everything beyond them had become a corridor with no doors.

The car stood beneath the same roof where Jolo had once balanced his phone on a jar of bolts. Ruel had repaired what could be repaired. The bodywork carried dents and replaced panels; the interior smelled of old water and fresh adhesive. The damaged camera card and the small data logger sat in a plastic evidence bag on the workbench. Ruel had kept them because he did not know what else to do with the parts of that night.

“Sit,” he said. “They've waited this long.”

Pang looked at the bag. “Did they find the other driver?”

Ruel's expression tightened. “Report says no second car.”

“They weren't there.”

“No. Neither was I.” Ruel spoke carefully, and that care made Pang angrier than an argument would have. “A man buying cigarettes saw lights on the lower road. Nobody puts them near you.”

Pang walked around the car. The driver's door had been replaced. The steering wheel was not the one he remembered. He ran his thumb along the repaired seam in the dash. There was a small mark near the center console where Jolo had once struck it with his knuckles to demonstrate a beat for a video edit.

He had returned to the scene to prove he was not afraid. Within a week, he was asking the same questions wherever people gathered after dark. Who else had been on the mountain? Which driver had been invited late? Did anyone see a car beside them? Why had Dante not spoken about the lights? Why had so many people been ready to blame the weather before checking the footage?

Rumors came easily. A driver had been seen leaving the mountain in a dark car. Someone said Dante had arranged a private rivalry. Another insisted Ghost itself had appeared on the route that night, the anonymous driver trying to protect the benchmark. No one could show him proof. Everyone had a version that made the death feel like an event with a cause he could find and confront.

Pang began racing again, slowly at first, then with the same determination that had led him into the mountain. He told himself the runs were tests. He wanted to know whether his hands still worked, whether he could feel the car's weight move beneath him, whether the fear had taken away something he could not get back.

But after every run, he asked someone about the crash.

Ruel watched him prepare the car one evening. “The car tells you some things,” he said. “Not all of them.”

Pang tightened the strap on his helmet. “I'm not deciding anything.”

Ruel looked at him for a long moment. “Then why do you keep asking who did it?”

# **Chapter Eleven**

## ***The First Return***

The first race Pang entered after the crash was not a competition so much as an argument with his own hands.

The route was short, the other drivers familiar, the weather clear. He told himself the conditions were controlled. Before the start, he sat in the car and listened to the engine idle. The vibration travelled through the steering column into his palms. He had missed the sound more than he had expected. It felt like hearing a language he had once spoken fluently and now had to translate.

When the run began, he drove too carefully through the first bends. The car followed the road, but he could not stop waiting for the moment the road would disappear. He checked the mirrors where there was nothing to see. He braked early. He finished near the back of the group and climbed out with his jaw clenched.

A younger driver laughed kindly and said, “You'll get it back, bro.”

Pang nearly hit him.

Instead, he walked to the edge of the staging area and looked at the mountain outline beyond the city. He knew his hands had not forgotten everything. He also knew the car had done exactly what he asked of it. That should have been enough to reassure him. It wasn't.

Nico found him near the food stall. “You kept checking your mirror. On an empty lane.”

Pang stared at him. “You saw something?”

“I saw you looking.” Nico bought two bottles of water, offered one, and waited until Pang took it. “I've chased the Ghost Line more times than I'll admit.”

The name landed between them, suddenly heavy.

“You think it was Ghost?”

“I think the clip is strange.” Nico opened his phone. “I've been comparing the sectors. The line's good. Some sections are excellent. The total doesn't behave like one run. I assumed there was a setup trick or a technique I hadn't found.”

Pang leaned closer. Nico showed him notes: entry speeds, sector times, brief observations written after repeated attempts. The handwriting was compact and precise. It did not look like a conspiracy. It looked like someone doing the work.

“Could someone have driven it once?” Pang asked.

“I can't prove they didn't. But nobody has reproduced the combined pace. Not even close, unless you count the separate best sectors.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means we need the original footage. The versions going around have been cropped and re-encoded. There's no useful metadata left. Whoever made the clip had the files before they were stripped.”

Pang looked at Nico's notes until the numbers blurred. He had come back searching for a driver who had forced them off the road. Instead, he was being told that another part of the story—the part that had brought him to the mountain in the first place—might not be what it seemed.

“Why are you helping me?” he asked.

Nico did not answer immediately. “Because I don't like being beaten by something that doesn't exist. And because I don't think you remember that night clearly.”

It was the first honest thing anyone had said to Pang about the crash that did not sound like an accusation. That made it harder to dismiss.

# **Chapter Twelve**

## ***The Wrong Tuesday***

The memories returned through objects.

A wrench striking concrete brought back the afternoon Jolo had laughed at the hood latch. The sound of a camera shutter took Pang to the warehouse race. A gear change on the bypass made him remember an argument in the garage, though he could not tell whether it had happened before or after the clip went public. Each memory arrived with the confidence of a thing that had just occurred. Each one carried a detail that did not fit.

In one recollection, Jolo wore the shirt he had been wearing on the night Pang first tried to chase Ghost. In another, that same shirt was wet from a rainstorm that had happened days earlier. Pang remembered Ruel replacing a wheel before the Ghost Line challenge, then found a photograph dated a week later in which the old wheel was still on the car. He remembered an argument at the coffee shop as if it had taken place the night before the crash, but the messages on his phone placed it in a different month.

He began to write things down.

At first the notes were practical: dates, names, locations, which cars had attended which events. Then the pages filled with fragments of conversation. Jolo saying, “That's not what I meant.” Dante saying, “Nobody cares about the notes.” Nico asking whether a time could belong to a run that had never happened. Some phrases had survived more clearly than the events around them.

Pang returned to the coffee shop where Dante held court. The tables were arranged differently, but the same cars filled the parking lot, and the same arguments gathered around them. Dante saw him enter and lowered his voice with the person he was speaking to. That small pause was enough to make Pang decide he had found something.

“You knew the clip was edited,” Pang said.

Dante gestured toward an empty chair. “I knew it was a reference video. Everyone who saw it knew it wasn't an official record.”

“They're competing against the total.”

“They're competing against a target. The event says what the target is.”

“You called it Ghost Time.”

“Because that is what people called it.” Dante's patience was beginning to thin. “Look— I never said a car pushed you. I never sent anyone near you. I wanted a good event. I wanted people to come. They came because the clip interested them.”

“You knew the truth would make it less interesting.”

Dante studied him, and for the first time the polish fell away from his expression. “You want me to say I made the crash. I made a race. You drove, my friend.”

Pang felt the answer strike the exact place he had tried to protect. Dante had not absolved himself. He had placed a boundary around his guilt and invited Pang to do the same.

“What did you do after the crash?” Pang asked.

“I kept it from turning into a scandal. I said tragedy. Was I wrong?”

“You let the rumors run.”

Dante looked away toward the parking lot. “Which one should I have stopped? There were twelve.”

“The ones that cost you money,” Pang said.

He left before Dante answered. Outside, a group of younger drivers were watching the Ghost Line clip on a phone, arguing over whether the unseen driver had used a different gearbox. Pang stopped at the edge of the circle. For a moment, he saw the scene as he once had: a place where people could make something out of nothing, where a good run meant that the next month might be better than the last.

Then the clip looped, and the ghost car made the corner without a single hesitation.

# **Chapter Thirteen**

## ***The Backup Folder***

Ruel kept backups because he distrusted any technology that assumed a single copy was enough.

The old drive sat in a drawer beneath receipts and spare fuses. He had used it to preserve Jolo's camera files after a laptop began failing, then forgotten about it as the months accumulated. When Nico mentioned that the circulating Ghost Line clips had lost their original metadata, Ruel remembered the drive and brought it to the workbench.

“Half these files are damaged,” he said. “Don't get excited.”

They spent an evening connecting folders to dates. The archive was a jumble of ordinary life: clips of the car on a lift, Jolo testing audio levels, Pang complaining about a rattle, an unfinished video of Nico's race. Some files had been named by date; others by whatever joke Jolo had made at the time. They found a folder labeled GHOST\_LINE and several versions of the edit, each with a different title card.

Pang stared at the name. “Why would he have kept this?”

“Because he kept everything,” Ruel said. “He thought it might be useful later.”

They opened the earliest version. The line was smoother than a raw recording, but the title card was still present. Jolo's voice came from the beginning of the clip, amused and close to the microphone. He was talking about the camera mount. Then the car turned into the first sector.

Pang felt the hair rise along his arms. He knew the road. He knew the shape of the dashboard reflection. He knew the tiny vibration in the camera image when the car crossed the seam near the bend. He had seen the same angle from the passenger seat of his own car a hundred times, but he had never thought to look for it here.

Ruel opened a raw recording from the same date. The steering correction appeared before the bend: a small lift, a brief adjustment, then the car settled. Another file contained the same movement from a later practice session. The timing was different. The conditions were different. But the gesture belonged to the same pair of hands.

“...Those are my hands,” Pang said.

Ruel did not answer. He placed the raw file beside the circulating clip and synchronized the sound. The engine note, the camera's small movement, the reflection on the windshield—all matched.

Pang reached for the mouse and rewound the clip. Jolo laughed at something off-camera. Pang heard his own voice asking to try the section again. The sound was so ordinary that it nearly broke him.

The unknown driver was not a stranger. The car that had become a legend was his own. The footage had been recorded from the passenger seat, and the small correction that Nico had noticed in the old line was Pang's habit.

He sat back. The room seemed to move away from him.

For months, he had searched for the person whose presence he imagined at the fatal corner. He had asked strangers to identify a driver who could match the Ghost Line, as if the person who made the perfect run might also explain why Jolo had died. Now the mystery had turned inward. He was the unseen driver. The legend belonged to his own past.

“He posted it. No name,” Pang said at last. “He wanted people to see it.”

Ruel nodded toward the folder. “It looks that way.”

Pang watched the clip again, searching for a clue that would make the discovery easier to bear. The line flowed across the screen with impossible ease. It looked like him, but not like any run he remembered completing.

He had found Ghost.

He still had no idea how Ghost had driven that way.

# **Chapter Fourteen**

## ***The Seams***

Nico arrived the next morning with a laptop, a stack of handwritten notes, and the expression of a man who had stayed awake because a problem had refused to become simple.

They began with the road marker. In one segment of the Ghost Line, the pale sign before the middle bend stood intact. In another, the same sign was bent inward, its edge dark with rust. They checked the raw footage. The sign had been damaged between practice sessions. The two segments could not belong to one continuous drive.

The asphalt told the same story. One pass had a wet sheen across the inside edge. Another was dry and dusty. The engine speed dipped at a transition and repeated the same small fluctuation a moment later, despite the car appearing to accelerate without interruption. The telemetry counters reset between sectors. None of these facts was conclusive alone. Together, they were impossible to ignore.

Jolo had assembled the best parts of several runs.

The original project notes confirmed it. Beneath the first version of the edit, in Jolo's familiar compressed typing, was the label Pang had seen only for a moment in the garage: \*\*COMPOSITE LINE STUDY — BEST SECTORS / NOT ONE RUN.\*\* The combined target was the fastest entry, the cleanest middle, and the best exit gathered from separate attempts. Every piece was real. The completed performance was not.

Pang watched the source runs one after another. In the raw entry, he corrected too much and had to slow before the next section. In the middle, he missed his line and returned to the start. In the exit, he carried speed beautifully—but that pass had begun under different conditions and after a different approach. Jolo's cuts removed the parts where the driver had to be human.

The revelation did not make Pang less talented. It made his talent less mythical. He had done each of those things, and he had done them well. He had simply never done all of them together.

He remembered Jolo bent over the laptop, proud of the edit. He remembered the excitement in his friend's voice when the finished clip played. Then he remembered the night in the parked car after the combined-sector challenge, when Jolo had begun to confess and stopped.

Ruel found the earlier video in a separate folder. It showed that night from a wider angle. Pang was looking at the road, talking about how close he was to Ghost. Jolo sat beside him with the phone open. On the screen, the original notes were visible. Jolo began speaking; Pang's excitement drowned out the first few words. Jolo looked at him and closed the phone.

They watched the scene in silence.

“He could have told me,” Pang said.

“Yes,” Ruel answered.

“He knew what it had become.”

“Yes.”

Pang wanted Ruel to defend Jolo, to say that he had only been trying to help, that the lie had grown too quickly for him to stop it. Ruel did not offer that comfort. He did not accuse Jolo either. He let the facts remain what they were: Jolo had created the study honestly, watched it become a myth, and stayed silent because the myth was opening doors for them.

Pang felt anger first. Then something quieter came beneath it, the recognition that he could understand why Jolo had chosen silence without being able to forgive the choice.

The perfect run never happened. The target Pang had chased was not a record made by a driver better than him. It was a composite of his own best moments, arranged to remove every failure between them.

He looked at the paused image. The ghost car was mid-corner, smooth and certain, with no sign that it had ever needed to slow down and try again.

“Then why couldn't I beat myself?” he asked.

Nico closed the laptop halfway. “Yourself? You were racing an edit. With a number even the edit never did.”

“The one we'll pretend you meant to do,” Pang said, to nobody.

# **Chapter Fifteen**

## ***The Lower Switchback***

Finding the source of the Ghost Line did not explain the headlights.

Pang returned to the mountain with Nico and Ruel, not to race but to see the road in the light. The day was bright enough to flatten distances. The mountain folded in layers, one bend crossing above another, the lower road visible through gaps in the trees. From the pull-off, the route looked less like a ribbon climbing the hillside and more like a series of separate places that happened to share the same slope.

Nico held the surviving dash footage on a tablet. The final seconds were damaged, but the headlights appeared briefly between the trees before the image broke. Pang watched them move and felt his body prepare for the old impact. The light crossed the windshield at the same angle he remembered.

“From here,” Nico said, pointing down through the branches, “the lower section runs behind that ridge. A car on it would be hidden for most of the bend, then its lights would show through the gap.”

They walked to a viewpoint farther along the road. From there, the geography made sense. The lower switchback curved back beneath the upper route. A vehicle moving through it could seem to travel beside someone on the road above, then vanish behind the terrain. It was an optical accident made by the mountain, the trees, and two sets of headlights.

A witness who had been leaving a small roadside stop on the night of the crash met them later that afternoon. He remembered a car on the lower road, though he could not recall its make or color. He had seen the lights move through the trees. He had not seen the car approach the upper bend, and he had no reason to think it had anything to do with the crash until people began asking.

“Sorry,” he said. “I thought the police knew more than me.”

Pang did not ask him to turn uncertainty into a statement. The man had seen what he had seen. That was all.

They returned to the garage with the route map and the recovered logger. Ruel had managed to extract part of the data: speed, braking, steering input, and several seconds of information beyond the point where the camera failed. The numbers were not a full reconstruction. They could not tell them every movement or prove what Pang had been thinking. But they recorded the car approaching the fatal corner faster than it had during the dry practice runs, braking later, then making a sharp steering correction as stability disappeared.

There was no sudden input consistent with avoiding a car entering his lane. No trace that required a collision to explain it. The car left the road after the correction, not before it.

Pang listened to the surviving audio. Jolo's voice, under the engine: “Pang, it's wet. It's wet.” Not the line Pang had carried for months. That one was from a dry Tuesday. Then the file ended in a burst of noise. The logger continued a little farther. The camera did not.

Ruel unplugged the logger and held it a moment. “Thirty years, every man who brings a car in here tells me what's wrong with it before I open the hood,” he said. “Almost always wrong. Not lying. They just tell me the version where it's the car's fault. I kept that box because the box doesn't do that. Not for you. Not for the family. I wanted one thing in all this that couldn't tell itself a story.”

“And now?”

“Now you know what it says.” He picked up a rag.


He could have stopped there and called it an answer. Instead, he asked Ruel to run the data again, checked the timestamps, and compared the traces with the road's slope and the tires on the car. Every test returned to the same broad conclusion: the surface had changed, the car had approached too quickly for those conditions, and Pang's correction had made the loss of grip worse.

It was not a perfect account of every second. It was the most coherent explanation the evidence allowed.

For months, Pang had preserved the headlights because they offered a person to blame. The light had crossed the road, and he had given it intention. Now the mountain stood in front of him without intention at all. It had only bends, weather, distance, and gravity. He had asked the road to behave like the recording, and when it did not, he had reacted to a threat he could not prove existed.

The answer was ordinary. That was what made it difficult to bear.

# **Chapter Sixteen**

## ***What the Numbers Can Say***

They spread the evidence across Ruel's workbench because there was no other table large enough.

The raw practice footage. The circulating Ghost Line. Jolo's project notes. Nico's sector comparisons. The damaged dash recording. The logger traces. The map of the lower switchback. The witness's statement, written in Ruel's careful hand. Individually, the pieces were incomplete. Together, they formed a story that did not require an invisible driver to make sense.

Pang stood over the papers and tried to arrange them into an accusation that would leave him untouched. Jolo had edited the footage. Dante had promoted the lie. The crowd had turned the benchmark into an obligation. He had been pressured, encouraged, sold a story, invited into a spectacle. Each statement was true in part. None changed the line recorded by the logger.

Nico ran a final series of controlled comparisons on a safe section of road. He used the same car where possible, the same start points, and repeated passes under labeled conditions. The point was not to recreate the fatal drive. It was to test the composite's logic. The best entry came from one pass, the middle from another, the exit from a third. When Nico tried to string them together, the time changed. The line needed different conditions and different compromises from one sector to the next.

“Every sector, you're close,” Nico said. “The number skips the road between them. You can't.”

Pang looked at the notes. “So it was impossible.”

“As advertised.” Nico tapped the first sector. “The middle was yours. The exit was yours. Write that down.”

That mattered more than Pang expected. He had been tempted to dismiss his own ability as another part of the lie. But the footage did not show a fraud. It showed a good driver with a good moment, then another good moment, then another—each separated by a mistake, a changed surface, or the patience to start over.

The myth had removed the work between them.

Pang took a marker and erased the phrase \*\*UNKNOWN DRIVER\*\* from the board. Beneath it, he wrote: \*\*WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS.\*\*

He listed four conclusions. The Ghost footage came from his own car. The benchmark was assembled from separate passes. The headlights could be explained by the lower switchback, and there was no credible evidence that another car forced them off the road. The logger supported the conclusion that he had approached the wet corner too quickly and lost control after a sharp correction.

He did not write that he knew every second. He did not write that no other factor could have mattered. The data could not tell them everything. It could tell them enough to stop treating a rumor as a fact.

Ruel stood across from him. “What now?”

Pang looked at the original notes, the line that Jolo had typed before anyone called it a legend. “I post the files. All of them.”

Nico nodded once. “Half of them won't believe it. I'm being generous.”

“I know.”

Pang capped the marker.

For the first time since the crash, he was not searching for an answer that could return him to the person he had been before it. He was trying to describe what had happened without giving himself a better role in it.

# **Chapter Seventeen**

## ***A Price for the Truth***

Dante read the project notes twice.

The first time, he was looking for a way to argue with them. The second time, he seemed to understand that the text was not going to change. Pang had brought the original files to the same coffee shop where the Ghost Line had first been celebrated. Outside, cars moved through the parking lot, their polished panels catching the late sun. The scene was still alive, still making plans for the next event. Nothing about it looked like a community preparing to admit it had been wrong.

“Coffee?” Dante said. “I'm getting coffee.”

The folder lay open between them: v1, title card and all. He did not pretend not to know it.

“The notes say 1:29.4. Your poster says 1:26.7.”

“Posters round.”

“Down?”

Dante smiled as if Pang had made a good point at a party. “Nobody drives down a mountain at night to chase 1:29.4, my friend. A record, somebody takes it, everybody goes home. A ghost sells this Saturday. And the one after.”

“You found time where there wasn't supposed to be any,” Pang said.

“I said that. To you. Behind the warehouses.” Dante seemed pleased to be remembered. “I didn't put you in the car. I didn't tell you to ignore the rain. I didn't cause the crash.”

“No,” Pang said. “You didn't.”

The admission seemed to surprise Dante more than an accusation would have. Pang had come expecting him to deny everything, perhaps to reveal some hidden arrangement that would put a person behind the headlights. Instead, the truth was uglier because it was smaller. Dante had not planned a death. He had chosen not to correct a lie that helped him make money, and after the crash he had allowed the legend to protect his reputation.

“I'm posting the files.”

“Post them. I won't stop it.” Dante stirred his coffee. “Then they'll ask who was driving that night, and you'll tell them that too. I'm only asking if you've thought about which part they'll remember.”

“I've thought about it.”

“Then you're braver than me. Sugar?”

Nico made the next decision. He published his sector notes, his repeated tests, and a measured explanation of why the combined pace could not be treated as a single-run record. He did not call Dante a criminal or claim to know why the crash happened. He said only what his evidence supported. That restraint gave the report credibility, but it did not make it popular.

Some drivers accepted it. Others insisted that all racing legends were built from exaggeration and that the Ghost Line remained a useful challenge regardless of its origin. Dante lost standing with some organizers and kept it with others. The events continued, though the name Ghost no longer filled every conversation.

Pang watched the discussion unfold without entering it. Forty-one people read Nico's post. Buko asked again on Thursday who Ghost was.

One final thing remained, and no file could do it for him.

# **Chapter Eighteen**

## ***Jolo’s Family***

Jolo's mother opened the door and looked at Pang for a long time before stepping aside.

He had rehearsed what to say in the car. He had prepared a sequence of facts and brought the original footage on a drive, along with the project notes and the copies of the surviving camera file. In his head, he had arranged the conversation as neatly as Jolo had once arranged the video: one point leading cleanly to the next, each detail supporting the conclusion, no gaps where a person might misunderstand him.

Inside, the house smelled of rice and laundry soap. A fan clicked in the corner. On a shelf stood the picture Jolo had taken at the talyer, the three of them, Ruel frowning. Pang could not look at it for long.

“I brought the original files,” he said.

Jolo's mother sat at the table. His father remained by the window, arms folded. Jolo's sister sat on the stairs. They did not offer him a drink. Pang set the drive down.

“The Ghost Line was cut from three of my runs. He labelled it a study. Dante took a number off it and posted it.” He made himself go on. “The lights were a car on the lower road. Not beside us. It was raining. Ruel said the tires were done. I went into the corner too fast. Jolo told me it was wet. I heard him.”

Nobody answered. Jolo's sister got up and left the room.

His father stared at the table. “So he made it up. The video.”

“He made the video. I drove.”

His mother held the drive in her hand, turning it slowly as if it might have a side that explained more than the other. “Why did you think there was another car?”

“I saw lights. I needed them to be someone.”

“Needed?”

Pang looked down. “If it was someone, I could go and find him.”

The room did not soften around the confession. No one told him that Jolo had loved him, though Pang knew he had. No one said the accident was not his fault. His mother put the drive on the table.

“Leave a copy of everything,” she said. Then: “A man came about a lens.”

“Kuya Ben. I'll pay him.”

“Did he eat? That night.”

“Pan de sal. In the car.”

“That's not dinner.” She went into the kitchen. The rice cooker clicked. Pang did not get up.

“I won't ask you to forgive me,” he said.

His father finally looked at him. “Good.”

Pang left the house without an answer. Outside, the afternoon carried on around him: a motorcycle passing, a neighbor calling to someone across the street, the ordinary sounds of a place that had not stopped because one family had been broken. He sat in the car for several minutes before turning the key.

The engine started on the second try. He let it idle until the temperature settled, then drove away without checking the time.

# **Chapter Nineteen**

## ***The Road Is Not a Recording***

Nico asked him to drive the mountain road in daylight.

Not as a test. Not to recreate the crash. Not for an event or a video. “I want to see what it looks like at forty,” he said.

They went on a weekday morning when the road was quiet but not empty. A delivery truck moved slowly uphill. A family of four on one motorcycle passed them before a bend. The trees shone with the clean green brightness that rain left behind, and the asphalt changed texture where repairs had been made.

Pang drove without a timer. At first, he could not stop thinking in sectors. He saw the start of the Ghost Line in the approach to the first bend, then its idealized middle section, then the fast exit. The ghost car appeared in his mind with its pale, smooth trajectory. He knew exactly where it wanted him to place the car. The knowledge arrived as a reflex, not a choice.

When they approached the fatal corner, the road narrowed under the trees. A vehicle was coming in the opposite direction farther ahead, its windshield catching the morning light. The outside edge was darker where moisture remained in the shade. Pang felt the old hunger rise—the need to prove that the corner could be taken cleanly, that he could pass through it without flinching, that the crash had not taken the driver he had been.

The ghost line brightened for a moment, so vivid that it seemed to lie over the road itself. The perfect entry. The precise angle. The imaginary car already leaving the corner before the real car had arrived.

Pang slowed.

He chose a line that gave the oncoming vehicle space and kept his tires on the surface he could actually see. The car moved through the bend without drama. Nothing spectacular happened. No one cheered. The ghost continued ahead and vanished beyond the trees, still carrying the impossible speed of a recording built from separate moments.

Pang did not follow it.

On the other side of the corner, he pulled into a safe lay-by and switched off the engine. For a while, neither man spoke. Nico looked toward the trees, then back at him.

“That's it?” he asked.

Pang rested his hands on the wheel. “That's it.”

They drove the rest of the route at an ordinary pace. The car rattled over a rough patch, and Pang heard the familiar trim tapping against the dash. Instead of imagining the sound as a defect that might ruin a run, he felt a strange affection for it. The car was imperfect. It had always been imperfect. Its flaws were part of the information it gave him, not a challenge to his pride.

At the bottom of the mountain, Nico stopped for coffee. They talked about tires, a friend who needed help finding a part, and a new shop that had opened near the highway. They did not talk about forgiveness or closure. There was no need to turn the drive into a ceremony.

# **Epilogue**

## ***The Kid at the Garage***

The garage was full again by the time the rains returned.

A small group had gathered around a car with its front bumper removed. Someone was looking for a used sensor. Someone else was wrong about coilovers. Ruel moved between them with a rag in one hand and a list of jobs in the other. The old calendar had finally been replaced, but the workbench was still crowded with bolts, receipts, and tools that no one put back in the same place twice.

Pang spent more time there now. He helped where he could, took small jobs, and kept the car running without pretending it would ever become the build he once imagined. He still drove. He still liked the sensation of a corner arriving and the car settling beneath him.

A young driver arrived near closing. His car was clean and loud, its tires new enough to shine. He waited until the conversation paused, then asked the question that had outlasted the evidence.

“Has anyone beaten the Ghost Line yet?”

A few people laughed. Someone said they had heard of a driver who came within a tenth. Another said the whole thing was fake. Ate Liza said it wasn't, exactly. That took a while.

“So nobody ever beat it?”

Pang looked at the young driver's car. “Who does your tires?”

“They're fine.”

“They're not finished becoming dangerous.”

“What?”

At the bench, Ruel didn't look up. “Different thing.”

The rain began tapping against the roller door. Ruel reached up to pull it down partway, and the metal rattled in its track. Somewhere outside, a car revved, then moved away into the ordinary night.

Pang took his keys from the workbench. The engine started on the second turn. The trim tapped the dash. He let it settle and drove out into the wet street.
